\section*{INTRODUCTION:}

This project is based on polynomial regression. Polynomial regression is used to model the non-linear relationship between an input variable and a target variable. Instead of fitting a straight line, which is used when the data have a linear relationship, polynomial regression uses different powers of the input variable:

$$
y = w_0 + w_1x + w_2x^2 + \cdots + w_mx^m
$$

where \(m\) is the polynomial degree. This equation produces a flexible curve that can fit the model to the data.

The objective of this assignment is to fit polynomial regression models with different degrees on the given noisy dataset and compare their training and validation errors. Based on the validation performance, we select the polynomial degree that gives the best result. The selected model is then retrained using both the training and validation data together and finally tested on the test data to evaluate how well the model performs on unseen data.

\subsection*{Polynomial Regression}

In this experiment, three main steps are involved: feature scaling, polynomial basis construction, and least-squares fitting.

\textbf{Feature Scaling:}

Feature scaling is performed to bring the input values into a similar and manageable range before applying the polynomial transformation. In this experiment, \(x\) is standardized using the mean \(\mu_x\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_x\) calculated from the training data.

Scaling is important in polynomial regression because higher powers of the input are used. If the values are not scaled, terms such as \(x^6\), \(x^{10}\), or \(x^{15}\) can become very large and may cause numerical instability during calculations. The same mean and standard deviation calculated from the training data are also used to transform the validation and test data. This ensures that information from the validation and test sets is not used during model training.

\textbf{Polynomial Basis:}

A polynomial basis is used so that the model can learn a curved relationship instead of being limited to a straight line. For a polynomial of degree \(d\), the standardized input is represented using powers from \(0\) to \(d\). As the polynomial degree increases, the model becomes more flexible and can capture more complex patterns in the data. However, if the degree is too high, the model can become unnecessarily complicated and may start fitting the noise instead of the actual underlying pattern.

\textbf{Least Squares:}

Least squares is used to find the best values of the polynomial weights. It minimizes the difference between the actual \(y\) values and the predicted values from the polynomial model. In other words, it finds the weights that give the smallest overall squared error on the training data. These weights are calculated using the normal equation.

Therefore, in this experiment, feature scaling helps keep the calculations numerically stable, the polynomial basis allows the model to capture non-linear patterns, and least squares finds the polynomial coefficients that best fit the training data. The performance of the model is then evaluated using Mean Squared Error (MSE).



\textbf{Feature standardization.}
Only the training split is used to calculate the mean and standard deviation:
\begin{align}
  \mu_x &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}
            \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}x_i,
  &
  \sigma_x &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}
            \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}(x_i-\mu_x)^2}, \\
  z_i &= \frac{x_i-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
  &
  N_{\mathrm{tr}} &= 6{,}000.
\end{align}
The same $\mu_x$ and $\sigma_x$ are used to standardize the validation and
test inputs.

\textbf{Polynomial basis.}
For degree $d$, each standardized input is mapped to
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\phi}_d(z_i)
  = \begin{bmatrix}1 & z_i & z_i^2 & \cdots & z_i^d\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{equation}
The design matrix is
\begin{equation}
  X_d =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    1 & z_1 & z_1^2 & \cdots & z_1^d \\
    1 & z_2 & z_2^2 & \cdots & z_2^d \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    1 & z_N & z_N^2 & \cdots & z_N^d
  \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Model and prediction.}
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_i
  = \boldsymbol{\phi}_d(z_i)^T\mathbf{w}
  = \sum_{j=0}^{d}w_jz_i^j.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Least-squares solution.}
The weights minimize the mean squared training residual:
\begin{equation}
  J(\mathbf{w})
  = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}
    \lVert\mathbf{y}-X_d\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2.
\end{equation}
Setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equation and its solution:
\begin{align}
  X_d^T X_d\hat{\mathbf{w}} &= X_d^T\mathbf{y}, \\
  \hat{\mathbf{w}} &= (X_d^T X_d)^{-1}X_d^T\mathbf{y}.
\end{align}

\textbf{Mean squared error.}
For any split $\mathcal{S}$,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathcal{S}}(d)
  = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}
    \sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Effect of Polynomial Degree}

A low-degree polynomial may underfit the data because it does not have enough flexibility to represent the non-linear relationship between \(x\) and \(y\). For example, a degree-\(1\) polynomial can only produce a straight line, so it cannot adequately capture a complex curved pattern.

As the polynomial degree increases, the fitted curve becomes more flexible. A degree-\(3\) or degree-\(5\) polynomial can capture more of the curvature in the data, while higher-degree polynomials can model more detailed variations.

In the experiment, the fitted curves become increasingly flexible as the degree increases from \(1\) to \(15\). The training error generally decreases as the degree increases because the model has more parameters and therefore a greater ability to fit the training data. However, increasing the degree indefinitely is not necessarily beneficial because a very complex polynomial can start fitting the noise rather than the underlying relationship. Therefore, the polynomial degree must be selected based on validation performance rather than training error alone.

The degree is selected using the validation error:
\begin{equation}
  d^* = \underset{d\in\{1,2,\ldots,15\}}{\arg\min}\;
        \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{val}}(d) = 10.
\end{equation}

The training and validation MSE values generally decrease as the polynomial degree increases. This indicates that increasing the model complexity initially helps the model fit the data better.

For degree \(1\), the training MSE is \(1144.10\) and the validation MSE is \(1055.51\). At degree \(3\), these values decrease to \(961.71\) and \(901.58\), respectively. For degree \(5\), they further decrease to \(906.23\) and \(849.98\), respectively.

A major improvement can be observed around degrees \(6\)--\(8\). The validation MSE decreases from \(849.98\) at degree \(5\) to \(534.78\) at degree \(6\), \(467.47\) at degree \(7\), and \(348.96\) at degree \(8\). This shows that higher-degree polynomials are able to capture the non-linear patterns in the data better than the lower-degree models.

After degree \(8\), the improvement becomes very small. The validation MSE is \(348.63\) at degree \(10\), which is only slightly lower than \(348.96\) at degree \(8\). At degree \(15\), the validation MSE increases slightly to \(349.14\). Therefore, degree \(10\) was selected because it gives the lowest validation MSE among the tested degrees.

The training and validation MSE values are also fairly close to each other. This suggests that the selected model is not simply fitting the training data but is able to generalize reasonably well to unseen data.

\subsection*{Final Model Evaluation}

After selecting $d^*=10$, the training and validation samples are combined:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}
  = \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}},
  \qquad |\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}|=8{,}000.
\end{equation}
The standardization parameters and degree-10 weights are recalculated using
only $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}$. The resulting held-out test error is
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{test}}(10)=340.0071.
\end{equation}

After comparing the different polynomial degrees, degree \(10\) was selected because it gave the lowest validation MSE of \(348.63\).

For the final model, the training and validation datasets were combined to create a larger fitting dataset containing \(8{,}000\) samples. This gives the final model more data to learn from after the polynomial degree has already been selected.

The mean and standard deviation used for standardization were recalculated using only these \(8{,}000\) samples. After that, the weights of the degree-\(10\) polynomial were recalculated using the combined training and validation data.

The test set was kept completely separate and was used only for the final evaluation. The resulting test MSE was \(340.0071\).

The test MSE is slightly lower than the validation MSE of \(348.63\). This shows that the selected degree-\(10\) model performs well on the unseen test data for this particular split. Since the test data were not used during training or model selection, the test MSE provides a fair estimate of how well the final model can generalize to new data.

\section*{\centering \underline{Inference}}

The experiment shows that polynomial degree has a significant effect on model complexity and performance. Very low-degree polynomials underfit the data because they are too simple to capture its non-linear relationship. As the degree increases, the model becomes more flexible, and both training and validation errors initially decrease.

A major improvement was observed between degrees \(5\) and \(8\), indicating that the additional polynomial terms helped the model capture important non-linear patterns in the data. After this point, the improvement became very small. Degree \(10\) produced the lowest validation MSE of \(348.63\) and was therefore selected as the final model. Although the training MSE continued to decrease slightly for higher degrees, the validation MSE increased slightly at degree \(15\), showing that increasing model complexity does not always improve generalization.

The final degree-\(10\) model was retrained using the combined \(8{,}000\) training and validation samples and achieved a test MSE of \(340.0071\) on the held-out test set. The close validation and test performance suggests that the selected model generalizes well to unseen data for this dataset.

Overall, the experiment demonstrated the importance of choosing an appropriate model complexity instead of simply selecting the model with the lowest training error. I also learned how data shuffling, feature standardization, polynomial basis construction, least-squares fitting, validation-based model selection, and final test evaluation work together to build and evaluate a regression model.
